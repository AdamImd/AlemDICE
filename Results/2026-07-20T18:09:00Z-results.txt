  ### 1. Replace terminal contracts with milestone contracts

  A contract should describe a small state machine rather than only “complete task before lease expiry”:

  accepted
    → prerequisite acquired
    → destination reached
    → action started
    → verified progress
    → completed

  Each milestone should have a native-state verifier. Payments can be released incrementally for verified
  milestones, with the largest share reserved for completion.

  For example, a stone-gathering bid would explicitly require:

  acquire_pickaxe → navigate_to_stone → mine → inventory_stone_delta >= 1

  This would have exposed the infeasible stone assignment immediately.

  ### 2. Detect stalls within a few ticks

  The protocol currently waits too long before recovering from an ineffective assignment. Add:

  - A progress heartbeat every 3–5 ticks.
  - last_verified_progress_tick.
  - Structured failure reasons such as missing_tool, cannot_reach, waiting_for_peer, and
    incorrect_task_model.

  - Automatic renegotiation when no verified progress occurs within something like max(3, 0.25 × ETA) ticks.
  - One diagnostic/replanning opportunity, followed by cancellation or reassignment.
  - Lease extensions only after verified progress.

  Repeated ineffective actions—such as attempting to mine without a pickaxe—should trigger a contract-level
  warning before consuming dozens of ticks.

  ### 3. Optimize for successful mission progress

  The current lowest-ask rule is too weak. A better award objective is:

  [
  \text{bid value} =
  P(\text{complete before deadline})
  \times \text{downstream mission value}

  - \text{price}
  - \text{opportunity cost}.
    ]

  Completion probability should reflect:

  - Prerequisite availability.
  - Travel time.
  - Agent role and efficiency.
  - Current commitments.
  - Task-specific historical reliability.
  - Dependency and critical-path effects.
  - Coordination risk.

  At minimum, travel and commitment costs should not remain zero. A slightly more expensive bidder with the
  necessary tool and a credible plan should beat a cheap but infeasible bid.

  ### 4. Make bids include plans, not just acceptance

  The LLM presently accepts or declines a largely deterministic quote. Instead, require a bounded structured
  proposal:

  {
    "accept": true,
    "eta": 11,
    "price": 5,
    "prerequisites": ["pickaxe"],
    "milestones": [
      "obtain_pickaxe",
      "move_to_stone",
      "mine_stone"
    ],
    "confidence": 0.72,
    "failure_risks": ["pickaxe_unavailable"]
  }

  The runtime should reject bids whose prerequisites contradict observable state. The model should estimate
  feasibility, while deterministic checks constrain invalid or strategically nonsensical bids.

  ### 5. Introduce coalition and bundle contracts

  Some mission nodes are structurally inappropriate for independent single-agent auctions:

  - Crafting and transferring resources form dependency chains.
  - Synchronized mining requires multiple agents at one place and time.
  - Resource gathering may benefit from interchangeable contributors.

  For synchronized work, issue an atomic coalition contract containing:

  - Required number of agents.
  - Rendezvous location.
  - Rendezvous time window.
  - Individual roles.
  - All-or-none acceptance.
  - Coalition-level completion verification.

  For task chains, auction bundles such as gather wood → deliver wood → craft tool, rather than allocating
  each node without regard to downstream feasibility.

  ### 6. Define contribution and substitution semantics

  The run ended with wood distributed across two agents, but the awarded agent had not individually
  collected the required amount. The protocol needs an explicit choice:

  - If the objective is team output, verify pooled inventory and divide payment according to verified
    contributions.

  - If the objective is assignee-specific execution, provide a contract-transfer or subcontract mechanism.
  - If either is acceptable, record contribution events and let the sponsor approve substitution.

  Without this, useful team progress can produce zero settlement.

  ### 7. Give CU actual utility

  Visible CU cannot affect behavior while agents cannot spend it and no contracts settle. Possible spending
  mechanisms include:

  - Paying another agent for assistance.
  - Subcontracting part of an award.
  - Purchasing priority or bandwidth.
  - Buying task-relevant information.
  - Funding lease extensions.
  - Posting collateral or insurance.

  Until such a mechanism exists, CU should be described as accounting rather than an incentive. The key
  ablation should remain:

  1. Language-only coordination.
  2. Structured contracts with hidden CU.
  3. Structured contracts with visible, spendable CU.

  This separates the value of structure from the value of currency.

  ### 8. Reduce protocol overhead

  Market prompts should carry only:

  - Newly offered tasks.
  - Current assignment.
  - Changes since the previous observation.
  - Recent verified progress or failure.
  - Relevant nearby messages.

  Do not repeatedly include the full task catalog and accumulated inbox. Keep a stable cacheable prompt
  prefix, cap protocol history by task, and trigger market reasoning primarily on contract events rather
  than every physical-action tick.

  ## Recommended revised protocol

  A practical next version would be:

  1. Sponsor broadcasts a locally feasible task with prerequisites and mission value.
  2. Agents submit structured plan bids.
  3. Runtime filters infeasible bids.
  4. Sponsor selects by probability-adjusted mission value.
  5. Winner acknowledges the award and its first milestone.
  6. Verifier checks progress every few ticks.
  7. Progress extends the lease and optionally releases partial CU.
  8. Stalls cause diagnosis, renegotiation, or rapid reassignment.
  9. Contributions, substitutions, and coalition participation are recorded.
  10. Final payment occurs only after native-state completion verification.

  Before scaling to 100 agents, I would require the revised protocol to achieve three engineering gates on
  short solvable missions: most contracts settle, stalled contracts are detected within roughly five ticks,
  and protocol token overhead stays below about 15%. Then evaluate longer missions over paired seeds before
  introducing sparse networks, manager failures, and large populations.

  ### 1. Replace terminal contracts with milestone contracts

  A contract should describe a small state machine rather than only “complete task before lease expiry”:

  accepted
    → prerequisite acquired
    → destination reached
    → action started
    → verified progress
    → completed

  Each milestone should have a native-state verifier. Payments can be released incrementally for verified
  milestones, with the largest share reserved for completion.

  For example, a stone-gathering bid would explicitly require:

  acquire_pickaxe → navigate_to_stone → mine → inventory_stone_delta >= 1

  This would have exposed the infeasible stone assignment immediately.

  ### 2. Detect stalls within a few ticks

  The protocol currently waits too long before recovering from an ineffective assignment. Add:

  - A progress heartbeat every 3–5 ticks.
  - last_verified_progress_tick.
  - Structured failure reasons such as missing_tool, cannot_reach, waiting_for_peer, and
    incorrect_task_model.

  - Automatic renegotiation when no verified progress occurs within something like max(3, 0.25 × ETA) ticks.
  - One diagnostic/replanning opportunity, followed by cancellation or reassignment.
  - Lease extensions only after verified progress.

  Repeated ineffective actions—such as attempting to mine without a pickaxe—should trigger a contract-level
  warning before consuming dozens of ticks.

  ### 3. Optimize for successful mission progress

  The current lowest-ask rule is too weak. A better award objective is:

  [
  \text{bid value} =
  P(\text{complete before deadline})
  \times \text{downstream mission value}

  - \text{price}
  - \text{opportunity cost}.
    ]

  Completion probability should reflect:

  - Prerequisite availability.
  - Travel time.
  - Agent role and efficiency.
  - Current commitments.
  - Task-specific historical reliability.
  - Dependency and critical-path effects.
  - Coordination risk.

  At minimum, travel and commitment costs should not remain zero. A slightly more expensive bidder with the
  necessary tool and a credible plan should beat a cheap but infeasible bid.

  ### 4. Make bids include plans, not just acceptance

  The LLM presently accepts or declines a largely deterministic quote. Instead, require a bounded structured
  proposal:

  {
    "accept": true,
    "eta": 11,
    "price": 5,
    "prerequisites": ["pickaxe"],
    "milestones": [
      "obtain_pickaxe",
      "move_to_stone",
      "mine_stone"
    ],
    "confidence": 0.72,
    "failure_risks": ["pickaxe_unavailable"]
  }

  The runtime should reject bids whose prerequisites contradict observable state. The model should estimate
  feasibility, while deterministic checks constrain invalid or strategically nonsensical bids.

  ### 5. Introduce coalition and bundle contracts

  Some mission nodes are structurally inappropriate for independent single-agent auctions:

  - Crafting and transferring resources form dependency chains.
  - Synchronized mining requires multiple agents at one place and time.
  - Resource gathering may benefit from interchangeable contributors.

  For synchronized work, issue an atomic coalition contract containing:

  - Required number of agents.
  - Rendezvous location.
  - Rendezvous time window.
  - Individual roles.
  - All-or-none acceptance.
  - Coalition-level completion verification.

  For task chains, auction bundles such as gather wood → deliver wood → craft tool, rather than allocating
  each node without regard to downstream feasibility.

  ### 6. Define contribution and substitution semantics

  The run ended with wood distributed across two agents, but the awarded agent had not individually
  collected the required amount. The protocol needs an explicit choice:

  - If the objective is team output, verify pooled inventory and divide payment according to verified
    contributions.

  - If the objective is assignee-specific execution, provide a contract-transfer or subcontract mechanism.
  - If either is acceptable, record contribution events and let the sponsor approve substitution.

  Without this, useful team progress can produce zero settlement.

  ### 7. Give CU actual utility

  Visible CU cannot affect behavior while agents cannot spend it and no contracts settle. Possible spending
  mechanisms include:

  - Paying another agent for assistance.
  - Subcontracting part of an award.
  - Purchasing priority or bandwidth.
  - Buying task-relevant information.
  - Funding lease extensions.
  - Posting collateral or insurance.

  Until such a mechanism exists, CU should be described as accounting rather than an incentive. The key
  ablation should remain:

  1. Language-only coordination.
  2. Structured contracts with hidden CU.
  3. Structured contracts with visible, spendable CU.

  This separates the value of structure from the value of currency.

  ### 8. Reduce protocol overhead

  Market prompts should carry only:

  - Newly offered tasks.
  - Current assignment.
  - Changes since the previous observation.
  - Recent verified progress or failure.
  - Relevant nearby messages.

  Do not repeatedly include the full task catalog and accumulated inbox. Keep a stable cacheable prompt
  prefix, cap protocol history by task, and trigger market reasoning primarily on contract events rather
  than every physical-action tick.

  ## Recommended revised protocol

  A practical next version would be:

  1. Sponsor broadcasts a locally feasible task with prerequisites and mission value.
  2. Agents submit structured plan bids.
  3. Runtime filters infeasible bids.
  4. Sponsor selects by probability-adjusted mission value.
  5. Winner acknowledges the award and its first milestone.
  6. Verifier checks progress every few ticks.
  7. Progress extends the lease and optionally releases partial CU.
  8. Stalls cause diagnosis, renegotiation, or rapid reassignment.
  9. Contributions, substitutions, and coalition participation are recorded.
  10. Final payment occurs only after native-state completion verification.

  Before scaling to 100 agents, I would require the revised protocol to achieve three engineering gates on
  short solvable missions: most contracts settle, stalled contracts are detected within roughly five ticks,
  and protocol token overhead stays below about 15%. Then evaluate longer missions over paired seeds before
  introducing sparse networks, manager failures, and large populations.

  ### Free-form language coordination

  Alem’s original approach asks LLM agents to infer roles, communicate, and act through language. This
  preserves generality, but language does not itself guarantee agreement, exclusivity, deadlines, or
  synchronized execution. The Alem benchmark’s authors identify communication as important while also
  finding coordination to remain a distinct long-horizon bottleneck. Alem paper
  (https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08340v1)

  Language is therefore valuable, but better used as a semantic layer:

  - Interpret unstructured human goals.
  - Explain failures.
  - Propose new task decompositions.
  - Negotiate situations not represented by the formal protocol.

  It should not carry routine bookkeeping such as leases, acknowledgments, progress status, and conflict
  resolution.

  ### Contract Net

  Contract Net was designed for cooperative task sharing through announcement, bidding, award, and
  execution. It distributes control and avoids a single allocation bottleneck. Smith’s original Contract Net
  paper (https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs631/papers/smith80contract.pdf)

  Its limitations closely match the current results:

  - Individual tasks are considered more readily than task bundles.
  - Bids are only as useful as their cost estimates.
  - It does not inherently model prerequisite chains.
  - Coalition formation is awkward.
  - Awarding a task does not ensure that an agent knows how to execute it.
  - Reauctioning is reactive and can be slow.

  Thus, Contract Net is not a bad choice—it is simply being asked to solve more than it naturally
  represents.

  ### Centralized optimization

  A centralized planner can formulate the problem as assignment, scheduling, or mixed-integer optimization:

  [
  \max \sum_{i,t} x_{it} U(i,t)
  ]

  subject to capacity, prerequisite, timing, tool, location, and coalition constraints.

  This is likely to outperform decentralized bidding when global state is available and there are only three
  agents. Multi-robot task-allocation literature explicitly connects task allocation to optimal assignment,
  while noting that temporal, spatial, heterogeneous, and cooperative requirements make richer variants
  difficult. Gerkey and Matarić’s MRTA taxonomy (https://ai.stanford.edu/~gerkey/research/mrta.html),
  dynamic spatial and temporal task allocation
  (https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/8889/8889-13-12417-1-2-20201228.pdf)

  For this project, centralized optimization is valuable even if decentralization is the research goal. It
  provides:

  - An oracle or upper-bound baseline.
  - Ground-truth assignments for diagnosing bid quality.
  - A way to separate allocation errors from execution errors.

  It is probably the best immediate method for three cooperative agents, but not necessarily the intended
  solution for 100 locally communicating agents.

  ### Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm

  CBBA extends auctions by letting agents bid on ordered bundles rather than isolated jobs. Agents
  repeatedly reconcile conflicting assignments through local consensus. Under its assumptions, it converges
  to conflict-free allocations and offers performance guarantees; it was designed to tolerate inconsistent
  situational awareness and changing communication topology. Choi, Brunet, and How
  (https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2009.2022423)

  This is a better match than plain Contract Net because an agent can value:

  collect wood → deliver wood → craft pickaxe

  as a bundle. Its marginal bid can incorporate how adding a task changes the agent’s existing schedule.

  However, standard CBBA still needs extensions for Alem:

  - Precedence-constrained bundles.
  - Tasks requiring simultaneous coalitions.
  - Dynamic task discovery.
  - Failed execution and replanning.
  - Uncertain LLM competence.
  - Non-submodular mission rewards.

  I would make an asynchronous, precedence-aware CBBA variant the principal decentralized allocator.

  ### Distributed Constraint Optimization

  DCOP represents agents, decision variables, hard or soft constraints, and a shared objective. Algorithms
  such as Max-Sum coordinate through messages over a constraint graph. This is attractive when one decision
  directly constrains another—for example, three agents must arrive at one location within the same time
  window. DCOP is a general framework for distributed coordinated decision-making, though exact solution is
  generally computationally hard. Structured-resource DCOP
  (https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2009/pdf/01_Full%20Papers/16_91_FP_0708.pdf), Max/Min-Sum DCOP
  (https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2012/papers/2B_2.pdf)

  Using DCOP for every movement would be excessive. It would be valuable as a local solver for:

  - Coalition selection.
  - Rendezvous scheduling.
  - Tool/resource constraints.
  - Conflicting task commitments.
  - Joint-action timing.

  In other words, CBBA could allocate ordinary bundles, while a DCOP solver handles the tightly coupled
  portions.

  ### Hierarchical task planning

  Alem missions are naturally hierarchical:

  obtain tool
  ├── gather required materials
  ├── transfer materials
  └── craft tool

  mine synchronized resource
  ├── recruit coalition
  ├── rendezvous
  └── perform simultaneous action

  Hierarchical planning makes these relationships explicit and can adapt a plan at the task, action,
  resource, or schedule level. Decentralized hierarchical planning has specifically been studied as a way to
  limit the ripple effects of replanning under uncertainty. LAPLATA decentralized HTN planning
  (https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5006/4879)

  The current mission DAG is already a partial version of this. The next step is to represent alternative
  methods, prerequisites, resource flows, and coalition templates—not merely ready/not-ready task nodes.

  ### Blackboard and stigmergic coordination

  In stigmergic systems, agents coordinate through changes to the environment or a shared artifact rather
  than continual direct conversation. This can scale well for:

  - Map discoveries.
  - Resource locations.
  - Hazard warnings.
  - Areas already searched.
  - Demand signals.
  - Congestion.

  Stigmergy has been used for decentralized coordination without direct pairwise messaging, including
  multi-agent control and exploration. Stigmergic coordination overview
  (https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/multi-agent-coordination-and-control-using-stigmergy/)

  Alem already has an environment through which agents indirectly interact. A structured shared blackboard
  could substantially reduce LLM communication costs. It is less suitable for commitments such as “three
  agents act at tick 180,” so it should complement rather than replace contracts.

  ### Multi-agent reinforcement learning

  Centralized training with decentralized execution learns agent policies using global information during
  training but local observations during deployment. QMIX, for example, learns a centralized joint value
  while preserving decentralized action selection. QMIX (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/rashid18a.html)

  MARL is attractive for:

  - Navigation.
  - Tool use.
  - Rendezvous behavior.
  - Learning when communication is useful.
  - Low-level synchronized actions.

  Its disadvantages are the very large training requirement and potentially poor zero-shot generalization.
  Alem’s reported 1-billion-step MARL result demonstrates that this is a meaningful performance comparison,
  but it answers a different question from whether pretrained models can coordinate without environment-
  specific training.

  A good system could use an LLM for mission interpretation and unfamiliar situations while learned or
  deterministic policies perform routine embodied actions.

  Native-state verification and blackboard
         ↺ failure-driven LLM replanning

  The division of responsibility should be:

  - LLM: interpret, decompose novel goals, reason about exceptions.
  - Hierarchical planner: maintain mission dependencies and alternative methods.
  - CBBA: allocate bundles using local communication.
  - DCOP/coalition solver: coordinate tightly coupled tasks.
  - Executor: reliably navigate and perform actions.
  - Verifier: measure actual progress.
  - Blackboard: distribute compact environmental facts.
  - CU: optionally implement incentives, reputation, assistance, or scarce-resource pricing.

  ## What should replace the current auction rule?

  Instead of selecting the lowest CU ask for each isolated task, each agent should construct a feasible
  schedule and calculate a marginal bundle bid:

  [
  \Delta U =
  P(\text{bundle succeeds})
  \cdot \Delta V_{\text{mission}}
  -\Delta C_{\text{travel}}
  -\Delta C_{\text{execution}}
  -\Delta C_{\text{coordination}}.
  ]

  Hard constraints should reject bids that lack:

  - Required tools.
  - Reachability.
  - Enough remaining time.
  - Required coalition partners.
  - A valid ordering with existing commitments.

  LLMs can help estimate unknown feasibility and formulate plans, but deterministic state should enforce
  every constraint it can.

  ### 1. Replace terminal contracts with milestone contracts

  A contract should describe a small state machine rather than only “complete task before lease expiry”:

  accepted
    → prerequisite acquired
    → destination reached
    → action started
    → verified progress
    → completed

  Each milestone should have a native-state verifier. Payments can be released incrementally for verified
  milestones, with the largest share reserved for completion.

  For example, a stone-gathering bid would explicitly require:

  acquire_pickaxe → navigate_to_stone → mine → inventory_stone_delta >= 1

  This would have exposed the infeasible stone assignment immediately.

  ### 2. Detect stalls within a few ticks

  The protocol currently waits too long before recovering from an ineffective assignment. Add:

  - A progress heartbeat every 3–5 ticks.
  - last_verified_progress_tick.
  - Structured failure reasons such as missing_tool, cannot_reach, waiting_for_peer, and
    incorrect_task_model.

  - Automatic renegotiation when no verified progress occurs within something like max(3, 0.25 × ETA) ticks.
  - One diagnostic/replanning opportunity, followed by cancellation or reassignment.
  - Lease extensions only after verified progress.

  Repeated ineffective actions—such as attempting to mine without a pickaxe—should trigger a contract-level
  warning before consuming dozens of ticks.

  ### 3. Optimize for successful mission progress

  The current lowest-ask rule is too weak. A better award objective is:

  [
  \text{bid value} =
  P(\text{complete before deadline})
  \times \text{downstream mission value}

  - \text{price}
  - \text{opportunity cost}.
    ]

  Completion probability should reflect:

  - Prerequisite availability.
  - Travel time.
  - Agent role and efficiency.
  - Current commitments.
  - Task-specific historical reliability.
  - Dependency and critical-path effects.
  - Coordination risk.

  At minimum, travel and commitment costs should not remain zero. A slightly more expensive bidder with the
  necessary tool and a credible plan should beat a cheap but infeasible bid.

  ### 4. Make bids include plans, not just acceptance

  The LLM presently accepts or declines a largely deterministic quote. Instead, require a bounded structured
  proposal:

  {
    "accept": true,
    "eta": 11,
    "price": 5,
    "prerequisites": ["pickaxe"],
    "milestones": [
      "obtain_pickaxe",
      "move_to_stone",
      "mine_stone"
    ],
    "confidence": 0.72,
    "failure_risks": ["pickaxe_unavailable"]
  }

  The runtime should reject bids whose prerequisites contradict observable state. The model should estimate
  feasibility, while deterministic checks constrain invalid or strategically nonsensical bids.

  ### 5. Introduce coalition and bundle contracts

  Some mission nodes are structurally inappropriate for independent single-agent auctions:

  - Crafting and transferring resources form dependency chains.
  - Synchronized mining requires multiple agents at one place and time.
  - Resource gathering may benefit from interchangeable contributors.

  For synchronized work, issue an atomic coalition contract containing:

  - Required number of agents.
  - Rendezvous location.
  - Rendezvous time window.
  - Individual roles.
  - All-or-none acceptance.
  - Coalition-level completion verification.

  For task chains, auction bundles such as gather wood → deliver wood → craft tool, rather than allocating
  each node without regard to downstream feasibility.

  ### 6. Define contribution and substitution semantics

  The run ended with wood distributed across two agents, but the awarded agent had not individually
  collected the required amount. The protocol needs an explicit choice:

  - If the objective is team output, verify pooled inventory and divide payment according to verified
    contributions.

  - If the objective is assignee-specific execution, provide a contract-transfer or subcontract mechanism.
  - If either is acceptable, record contribution events and let the sponsor approve substitution.

  Without this, useful team progress can produce zero settlement.

  ### 7. Give CU actual utility

  Visible CU cannot affect behavior while agents cannot spend it and no contracts settle. Possible spending
  mechanisms include:

  - Paying another agent for assistance.
  - Subcontracting part of an award.
  - Purchasing priority or bandwidth.
  - Buying task-relevant information.
  - Funding lease extensions.
  - Posting collateral or insurance.

  Until such a mechanism exists, CU should be described as accounting rather than an incentive. The key
  ablation should remain:

  1. Language-only coordination.
  2. Structured contracts with hidden CU.
  3. Structured contracts with visible, spendable CU.

  This separates the value of structure from the value of currency.

  ### 8. Reduce protocol overhead

  Market prompts should carry only:

  - Newly offered tasks.
  - Current assignment.
  - Changes since the previous observation.
  - Recent verified progress or failure.
  - Relevant nearby messages.

  Do not repeatedly include the full task catalog and accumulated inbox. Keep a stable cacheable prompt
  prefix, cap protocol history by task, and trigger market reasoning primarily on contract events rather
  than every physical-action tick.

  ## Recommended revised protocol

  A practical next version would be:

  1. Sponsor broadcasts a locally feasible task with prerequisites and mission value.
  2. Agents submit structured plan bids.
  3. Runtime filters infeasible bids.
  4. Sponsor selects by probability-adjusted mission value.
  5. Winner acknowledges the award and its first milestone.
  6. Verifier checks progress every few ticks.
  7. Progress extends the lease and optionally releases partial CU.
  8. Stalls cause diagnosis, renegotiation, or rapid reassignment.
  9. Contributions, substitutions, and coalition participation are recorded.
  10. Final payment occurs only after native-state completion verification.

  Before scaling to 100 agents, I would require the revised protocol to achieve three engineering gates on
  short solvable missions: most contracts settle, stalled contracts are detected within roughly five ticks,
  and protocol token overhead stays below about 15%. Then evaluate longer missions over paired seeds before
  introducing sparse networks, manager failures, and large populations.

  ### Free-form language coordination

  Alem’s original approach asks LLM agents to infer roles, communicate, and act through language. This
  preserves generality, but language does not itself guarantee agreement, exclusivity, deadlines, or
  synchronized execution. The Alem benchmark’s authors identify communication as important while also
  finding coordination to remain a distinct long-horizon bottleneck. Alem paper
  (https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08340v1)

  Language is therefore valuable, but better used as a semantic layer:

  - Interpret unstructured human goals.
  - Explain failures.
  - Propose new task decompositions.
  - Negotiate situations not represented by the formal protocol.

  It should not carry routine bookkeeping such as leases, acknowledgments, progress status, and conflict
  resolution.

  ### Contract Net

  Contract Net was designed for cooperative task sharing through announcement, bidding, award, and
  execution. It distributes control and avoids a single allocation bottleneck. Smith’s original Contract Net
  paper (https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs631/papers/smith80contract.pdf)

  Its limitations closely match the current results:

  - Individual tasks are considered more readily than task bundles.
  - Bids are only as useful as their cost estimates.
  - It does not inherently model prerequisite chains.
  - Coalition formation is awkward.
  - Awarding a task does not ensure that an agent knows how to execute it.
  - Reauctioning is reactive and can be slow.

  Thus, Contract Net is not a bad choice—it is simply being asked to solve more than it naturally
  represents.

  ### Centralized optimization

  A centralized planner can formulate the problem as assignment, scheduling, or mixed-integer optimization:

  [
  \max \sum_{i,t} x_{it} U(i,t)
  ]

  subject to capacity, prerequisite, timing, tool, location, and coalition constraints.

  This is likely to outperform decentralized bidding when global state is available and there are only three
  agents. Multi-robot task-allocation literature explicitly connects task allocation to optimal assignment,
  while noting that temporal, spatial, heterogeneous, and cooperative requirements make richer variants
  difficult. Gerkey and Matarić’s MRTA taxonomy (https://ai.stanford.edu/~gerkey/research/mrta.html),
  dynamic spatial and temporal task allocation
  (https://cdn.aaai.org/ojs/8889/8889-13-12417-1-2-20201228.pdf)

  For this project, centralized optimization is valuable even if decentralization is the research goal. It
  provides:

  - An oracle or upper-bound baseline.
  - Ground-truth assignments for diagnosing bid quality.
  - A way to separate allocation errors from execution errors.

  It is probably the best immediate method for three cooperative agents, but not necessarily the intended
  solution for 100 locally communicating agents.

  ### Consensus-Based Bundle Algorithm

  CBBA extends auctions by letting agents bid on ordered bundles rather than isolated jobs. Agents
  repeatedly reconcile conflicting assignments through local consensus. Under its assumptions, it converges
  to conflict-free allocations and offers performance guarantees; it was designed to tolerate inconsistent
  situational awareness and changing communication topology. Choi, Brunet, and How
  (https://doi.org/10.1109/TRO.2009.2022423)

  This is a better match than plain Contract Net because an agent can value:

  collect wood → deliver wood → craft pickaxe

  as a bundle. Its marginal bid can incorporate how adding a task changes the agent’s existing schedule.

  However, standard CBBA still needs extensions for Alem:

  - Precedence-constrained bundles.
  - Tasks requiring simultaneous coalitions.
  - Dynamic task discovery.
  - Failed execution and replanning.
  - Uncertain LLM competence.
  - Non-submodular mission rewards.

  I would make an asynchronous, precedence-aware CBBA variant the principal decentralized allocator.

  ### Distributed Constraint Optimization

  DCOP represents agents, decision variables, hard or soft constraints, and a shared objective. Algorithms
  such as Max-Sum coordinate through messages over a constraint graph. This is attractive when one decision
  directly constrains another—for example, three agents must arrive at one location within the same time
  window. DCOP is a general framework for distributed coordinated decision-making, though exact solution is
  generally computationally hard. Structured-resource DCOP
  (https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2009/pdf/01_Full%20Papers/16_91_FP_0708.pdf), Max/Min-Sum DCOP
  (https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2012/papers/2B_2.pdf)

  Using DCOP for every movement would be excessive. It would be valuable as a local solver for:

  - Coalition selection.
  - Rendezvous scheduling.
  - Tool/resource constraints.
  - Conflicting task commitments.
  - Joint-action timing.

  In other words, CBBA could allocate ordinary bundles, while a DCOP solver handles the tightly coupled
  portions.

  ### Hierarchical task planning

  Alem missions are naturally hierarchical:

  obtain tool
  ├── gather required materials
  ├── transfer materials
  └── craft tool

  mine synchronized resource
  ├── recruit coalition
  ├── rendezvous
  └── perform simultaneous action

  Hierarchical planning makes these relationships explicit and can adapt a plan at the task, action,
  resource, or schedule level. Decentralized hierarchical planning has specifically been studied as a way to
  limit the ripple effects of replanning under uncertainty. LAPLATA decentralized HTN planning
  (https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/5006/4879)

  The current mission DAG is already a partial version of this. The next step is to represent alternative
  methods, prerequisites, resource flows, and coalition templates—not merely ready/not-ready task nodes.

  ### Blackboard and stigmergic coordination

  In stigmergic systems, agents coordinate through changes to the environment or a shared artifact rather
  than continual direct conversation. This can scale well for:

  - Map discoveries.
  - Resource locations.
  - Hazard warnings.
  - Areas already searched.
  - Demand signals.
  - Congestion.

  Stigmergy has been used for decentralized coordination without direct pairwise messaging, including
  multi-agent control and exploration. Stigmergic coordination overview
  (https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/multi-agent-coordination-and-control-using-stigmergy/)

  Alem already has an environment through which agents indirectly interact. A structured shared blackboard
  could substantially reduce LLM communication costs. It is less suitable for commitments such as “three
  agents act at tick 180,” so it should complement rather than replace contracts.

  ### Multi-agent reinforcement learning

  Centralized training with decentralized execution learns agent policies using global information during
  training but local observations during deployment. QMIX, for example, learns a centralized joint value
  while preserving decentralized action selection. QMIX (https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/rashid18a.html)

  MARL is attractive for:

  - Navigation.
  - Tool use.
  - Rendezvous behavior.
  - Learning when communication is useful.
  - Low-level synchronized actions.

  Its disadvantages are the very large training requirement and potentially poor zero-shot generalization.
  Alem’s reported 1-billion-step MARL result demonstrates that this is a meaningful performance comparison,
  but it answers a different question from whether pretrained models can coordinate without environment-
  specific training.

  A good system could use an LLM for mission interpretation and unfamiliar situations while learned or
  deterministic policies perform routine embodied actions.

  ## Recommended architecture for Alem

  Human instruction
         ↓
  LLM mission interpreter
         ↓
  Hierarchical task and dependency graph
         ↓
  Precedence-aware CBBA allocator
         ↓
  ┌─────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ Ordinary task bundles              │
  │ Coalition/DCOP solver when needed  │
  └─────────────────────────────────────┘
         ↓
  Milestone contracts
         ↓
  Learned or deterministic executors
         ↓
  Native-state verification and blackboard
         ↺ failure-driven LLM replanning

  The division of responsibility should be:

  - LLM: interpret, decompose novel goals, reason about exceptions.
  - Hierarchical planner: maintain mission dependencies and alternative methods.
  - CBBA: allocate bundles using local communication.
  - DCOP/coalition solver: coordinate tightly coupled tasks.
  - Executor: reliably navigate and perform actions.
  - Verifier: measure actual progress.
  - Blackboard: distribute compact environmental facts.
  - CU: optionally implement incentives, reputation, assistance, or scarce-resource pricing.

  ## What should replace the current auction rule?

  Instead of selecting the lowest CU ask for each isolated task, each agent should construct a feasible
  schedule and calculate a marginal bundle bid:

  [
  \Delta U =
  P(\text{bundle succeeds})
  \cdot \Delta V_{\text{mission}}
  -\Delta C_{\text{travel}}
  -\Delta C_{\text{execution}}
  -\Delta C_{\text{coordination}}.
  ]

  Hard constraints should reject bids that lack:

  - Required tools.
  - Reachability.
  - Enough remaining time.
  - Required coalition partners.
  - A valid ordering with existing commitments.

  LLMs can help estimate unknown feasibility and formulate plans, but deterministic state should enforce
  every constraint it can.

  ## Experimental comparison to run

  The strongest paper would not ask whether CU is “the best coordination method.” It would ask which
  components improve coordination and under what conditions.

  Compare:

  1. Language-only Alem.
  2. Static role-aware allocation.
  3. Centralized planner oracle.
  4. Plain Contract Net without CU.
  5. Milestone Contract Net without CU.
  6. Precedence-aware CBBA.
  7. CBBA plus coalition/DCOP handling.
  8. CBBA plus visible, spendable CU.
  9. MARL executor or published MARL baseline.

  Measure:

  - Mission score and survival.
  - Contract settlement rate.
  - Time to first verified progress.
  - Stall-detection and recovery time.
  - Allocation regret against the centralized oracle.
  - Messages, tokens, and wall time.
  - Performance under communication loss.
  - Performance under agent failure or deceptive bids.
  - Scaling from 3 to 8, 16, 32, and eventually 100 agents.

  The most defensible hypothesis is:

  > Structured hybrid coordination will outperform both unrestricted language communication and isolated-
  > task Contract Net by combining semantic adaptability with explicit dependency, coalition, and execution
  > guarantees.

  That is a stronger scientific position than claiming markets are inherently optimal. The interesting
  contribution is likely not CU alone, but determining when language, planning, auctions, shared state, and
  learned control should each be used.

  The important distinction is whether the boss sees legitimate shared information or privileged simulator

  ## Recommended architecture

  Use four logical agents:

                    Central planner
          sees objectives and team information
                /         |         \
           Worker 1    Worker 2    Worker 3

  The planner should:

  - Decompose the mission into a dependency graph.
  - Assign roles and tasks.
  - Detect missing prerequisites.
  - Coordinate transfers and rendezvous.
  - Monitor verified progress.
  - Reassign stalled tasks.
  - Resolve conflicting worker intentions.

  Workers should:

  - Observe their local environment.
  - Execute assigned milestones.
  - Report discoveries, progress, and blockers.
  - Retain local control over navigation and immediate survival.

  The boss should normally make decisions when an event occurs—completion, failure, discovery, timeout, or
  dependency change—not on every environment tick.

  ## Why it could fix the observed failures

  A central planner could have recognized that:

  - Mining stone required obtaining or crafting a pickaxe first.
  - The wood objective could be divided between workers or assigned to one worker explicitly.
  - Two partial wood contributions needed consolidation or team-level credit.
  - Three-agent mining required a coalition and rendezvous plan.
  - Repeated ineffective Do actions indicated a stalled plan.
  - A 24–36 tick lease should not remain active without progress.

  Contract Net distributed these decisions across agents with incomplete information and unreliable bid
  estimates. A central planner can reason over the complete task dependency graph and compare all workers
  simultaneously.

  ## Cleaner formalization

  With decentralized agents, Alem resembles a decentralized partially observable decision process: each
  agent observes only part of the environment and independently selects actions.

  With a central planner, define:

  - (s_t): information available to the planner.
  - (o_t^i): worker (i)’s local observation.
  - (g): mission objective.
  - (z_t^i): subgoal assigned to worker (i).
  - (a_t^i): physical action selected by worker (i).
  - (V(g)): mission achievement value.
  - (C): token, communication, and coordination cost.

  The planner selects subgoals:

  # [
  (z_t^1,z_t^2,z_t^3)

  \pi_{\mathrm{planner}}
  \left(
  g,s_t,h_t,
  {o_t^i,r_t^i}_{i=1}^{3}
  \right),
  ]

  where (r_t^i) is worker (i)’s progress report and (h_t) is team history. Each worker then acts locally:

  [
  a_t^i =
  \pi_i(o_t^i,z_t^i,h_t^i).
  ]

  The optimization objective becomes:

  [
  \max_{\pi_{\mathrm{planner}},\pi_1,\ldots,\pi_3}
  \mathbb{E}
  \left[
  V(g)
  +\lambda R_{\mathrm{survival}}
  -\alpha C_{\mathrm{tokens}}
  -\beta C_{\mathrm{communication}}
  \right].
  ]

  This separates two problems that are currently entangled:

  1. Strategic coordination: What should each agent do?
  2. Embodied execution: Can the assigned agent actually do it?

  That separation will make failures much easier to diagnose.

  ## Avoiding privileged-information confounds

  I recommend implementing two planner variants.

  ### Information-equivalent planner

  The planner receives:

  - The official mission objective.
  - Every worker’s legal observation.
  - Worker inventories and reported status, if normally shareable.
  - Verified task progress.
  - Communication history.

  It does not receive hidden map cells, object arrays, future state, or simulator internals unavailable to
  the workers.

  This is the scientifically fair treatment. It centralizes information and decisions, but does not grant
  omniscience.

  ### Omniscient oracle planner

  The planner receives full native simulator state and hidden-map information.

  This should be labeled an oracle or upper bound, not compared as an equivalent agent method. It answers:

  > How much performance is possible if coordination and information aggregation are solved?

  The difference between the oracle and information-equivalent planner estimates the cost of partial
  observability. The difference between the information-equivalent planner and workers’ realized behavior
  estimates execution loss.

  ## Suggested planning output

  Rather than issuing vague instructions, the planner should generate structured assignments:

  {
    "plan_version": 7,
    "objective": "craft_pickaxe",
    "assignments": [
      {
        "agent": "agent_0",
        "subgoal": "gather_wood",
        "amount": 2,
        "milestones": [
          "reach_tree",
          "collect_first_wood",
          "collect_second_wood"
        ],
        "deadline": 35
      },
      {
        "agent": "agent_1",
        "subgoal": "explore_for_stone",
        "milestones": ["locate_stone", "report_location"],
        "deadline": 25
      },
      {
        "agent": "agent_2",
        "subgoal": "maintain_survival_and_assist",
        "deadline": 25
      }
    ],
    "replan_conditions": [
      "no_progress_for_5_ticks",
      "required_resource_unreachable",
      "agent_health_below_threshold"
    ]
  }

  The environment should verify milestones and automatically notify the planner. Workers can report
  unexpected problems, but the system should not depend exclusively on self-reported success.

  ## Does CU remain necessary?

  Probably not for the primary three-agent cooperative formulation.

  If all agents share the same team objective and obey the central planner, artificial currency adds
  accounting and prompt overhead without solving an incentive conflict. CU makes more sense when:

  - Agents have different owners or private objectives.
  - Agents can refuse work strategically.
  - Capabilities or costs are private information.
  - Resources must be rationed.
  - Agents can purchase assistance or information.
  - Malicious and self-interested behavior is being studied.

  For fully cooperative workers, milestone verification, reliability estimates, and task reassignment are
  simpler than payments.

  CU could remain as a later experimental condition:

  > Does an incentive mechanism improve coordination when agents have heterogeneous or partially conflicting
  > objectives?

  That is a stronger motivation than attaching currency to agents already instructed to maximize the same
  team score.

  ## Limitations of a boss agent

  Central planning is likely advantageous with three agents, but it introduces:

  - A single point of failure.
  - A communication bottleneck.
  - Longer planner prompts as the population grows.
  - Potential worker over-reliance on incorrect plans.
  - Reduced robustness to network partitions.
  - Less genuinely decentralized or emergent behavior.

  For 100 agents, one planner reading every observation will probably become impractical. A hierarchy is
  more plausible:

                   Mission planner
                 /       |       \
            Team lead  Team lead  Team lead
              /|\         /|\        /|\
           workers      workers     workers

  Each team lead coordinates perhaps 5–10 nearby agents, while the top-level planner allocates mission
  regions, resources, and coalition goals.

  ## Best experimental framing

  A central planner should first be introduced as both a strong baseline and a candidate method:

  1. Language-only decentralized agents.
  2. Information-equivalent central LLM planner.
  3. Omniscient central oracle.
  4. Contract Net without CU.
  5. Contract Net with visible CU.
  6. Later: hierarchical planners at larger scales.
